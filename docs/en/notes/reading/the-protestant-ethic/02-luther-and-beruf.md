# II. Luther and the Calling

## 1. The word itself

One of Weber's sharpest observations: the modern European words for "occupation" all carry the undertone of a divine summons.

- German `Beruf`, Dutch `beroep`, English `calling`, French `vocation`;
- Whereas in languages dominated by Catholicism the corresponding term (e.g. `profession`) means only a secular trade.

The modern sense of `Beruf` was largely fixed by Luther's Bible translation — most often cited is his rendering of Sirach and of 1 Corinthians 7:20: a person should "remain" in the station God has assigned.

## 2. What Luther did

Luther's contribution is an **inversion of the hierarchy of values**:

| | Medieval Catholic hierarchy | Lutheran revaluation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Highest form | Monastic withdrawal (*evangelical counsels*) | Fulfilling one's duty in a worldly calling |
| Everyday labour | Worldly, inferior, inevitably tainted by sin | Ordained by God, to be performed faithfully |
| Route to salvation | Sacraments, confession, good works, church mediation | By faith alone; daily work is the believer's duty |

For the first time, "do your work well in the world" acquired a *positive* religious meaning: the calling is no longer an obstacle to the religious life — it *is* the religious life.

## 3. But Luther is not yet the spirit of capitalism

Here Weber applies the brakes. Luther's doctrine of the calling has a decisive limit:

- **Quietism**: the calling is the place God put you; you are to stay in it, not to better yourself or climb;
- **Traditionalism**: enough is enough — moderation and contentment, not endless expansion;
- Luther himself attacked usury and worldly profit-seeking in the strongest terms.

So Luther supplies the **foundation**, not the **engine**: he made worldly labour sacred, but gave no psychological drive towards ever-renewed gain and never-satisfied accumulation. A Lutheran could run his shop in peace — but he had no reason to plough profits back in and compress his life into a discipline.

> [!NOTE] Why the drive must be sought elsewhere
> Weber argues that only a stronger psychological mechanism than "quiet obedience" could turn daily labour into systematic, continuous, self-imposed conduct. Calvinist predestination supplied exactly that engine — the subject of the next entry.
