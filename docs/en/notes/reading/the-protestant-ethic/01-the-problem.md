# I. Weber's Question: Starting from Franklin

## 1. A statistical observation, and two traps

Weber opens with occupation statistics from Germany at the time: in Baden and elsewhere, Protestants were markedly over-represented among business owners, skilled workers and senior managers relative to their share of the population.

He immediately blocks the two cheapest explanations:

- Not "Protestants are more secularised" — the Reformation was itself a revolt against ecclesiastical domination, and early Protestantism was *stricter* than Catholicism, not looser;
- Not "they were richer, hence better educated" — the causality may run the other way; the question is precisely where the money came from in the first place.

So the question is forced into a sharper position: **does a religious disposition itself shape a particular economic ethic?**

## 2. A specimen of the spirit: Franklin

Weber dissects Benjamin Franklin's *Advice to a Young Tradesman* as his sample:

> Remember, that time is money… Remember, that credit is money… Remember, that money is of the prolific, generating nature.

What matters is not that these lines teach profit-making, but that they express an **ethic**:

- Money is made neither for consumption, pleasure nor display — making it is treated as an **end in itself**;
- Punctuality, credit, thrift, industry are **duties**, not tactics;
- Wasting time is a moral failing.

Weber stresses that this disposition is directed *against* greed:

| | The traditional money-maker | The spirit of capitalism |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Purpose | Money buys a more comfortable life | Money-making is itself an obligation |
| Consumption | Spend what you earn, even lavishly | Systematically hold personal consumption down |
| Time | Stop once you have enough | Continuous, monotonous, methodical labour |

So: the spirit of capitalism is not *stronger greed*, but a **rationalised and ethicised drive for profit**.

## 3. Four working concepts

| Concept | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Spirit of capitalism (*kapitalistischer Geist*) | An ethos that treats rational profit-making as a calling |
| Traditionalism | The "business as usual" economic mentality — the foil to the above |
| Ideal type (*Idealtypus*) | A method: distil and sharpen features from reality rather than copy it |
| Elective affinity (*Wahlverwandtschaft*) | Mutual attraction and reinforcement between ethic and institution — not one-way causation |

> [!IMPORTANT] Weber does not say Protestantism *caused* capitalism
> He explicitly rejects single-line causality. "Elective affinity" means a religious ethic and an economic mentality that fit and reinforce each other. Reading Weber as a cultural determinist gets the book exactly backwards.

## 4. Three threads to follow

1. How did Protestantism — Calvinism especially — turn ordinary labour into a religious obligation?
2. How did that sense of obligation become a behavioural mechanism of endless accumulation rather than endless consumption?
3. Once the religious drive disappears, what is left of the mechanism?
