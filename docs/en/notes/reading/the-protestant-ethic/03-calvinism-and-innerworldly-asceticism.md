# III. Calvinism and Innerworldly Asceticism

## 1. The core doctrine: predestination

Calvinism's most abrasive doctrine is **predestination** (*decretum horribile*, the "horrible decree"):

- God decided before creation who is saved (the elect) and who is damned;
- The decision is irrevocable and **cannot be affected by human conduct**;
- Sacraments, confession, good works, priestly mediation — the entire Catholic apparatus for granting assurance of salvation — are void.

Weber's interest is not the doctrine but its **psychological** consequences.

## 2. The resulting anxiety: how can I know I am elect?

A medieval Catholic could square the ledger again through confession. A Calvinist found himself in a room with no echo. "Assurance of salvation" (*certitudo salutis*) became the central problem of life.

The pastoral answer is the decisive move:

| Step | Content |
| --- | --- |
| Not "good works buy grace" | Grace cannot be bought; works cannot alter God's choice |
| But "good works prove election" | A strict, industrious, self-denying worldly life is read as a *sign* of being elect |
| Hence relentless self-examination | The believer must monitor conduct and intention continuously and systematically |
| Result | A self-imposed discipline that disciplines the whole of life |

A small but decisive shift in attitude: from "was I good enough today?" to "does my whole way of living show that I am among the elect?"

## 3. Innerworldly asceticism (*innerweltliche Askese*)

Calvinism's solution was to carry monastic discipline **out of the monastery**:

- Medieval monastic asceticism was **otherworldly** — leave the world, keep poverty, chastity and obedience behind the walls;
- Protestant asceticism is **innerworldly** — stay in the world, in family and calling, under the same discipline, expressed as industry, refusal of enjoyment, and methodical use of time.

Its practical maxims run roughly like this:

1. **Time**: idle talk, luxury, excess sleep, entertainment beyond what is necessary — all are losses of time God gave you;
2. **Labour**: work in one's calling glorifies God; industry is a duty, idleness a sin;
3. **Consumption**: wealth itself need not be condemned, but the *enjoyment* of wealth must be resisted — it tempts towards slackness and fall;
4. **Legitimate use of wealth**: not consumption, but reinvestment in further labour in one's calling.

> [!TIP] The single most important sentence
> "When the limitation of consumption is combined with this release of acquisitive activity, the inevitable practical result is obvious: accumulation of capital through ascetic compulsion to save." That is where the book's causal chain closes.

## 4. From ethic to mechanism

Chain the maxims together and a mechanism appears that runs even without the theology:

enjoyment forbidden → consumption pressed to the bare minimum → profit cannot be spent → it can only be reinvested → labour is sacralised and continuously intensified → capital accumulates

This is where Weber is most often misread: he is not describing "Protestants who are better at business", but a **psychological apparatus that converts desire into discipline, and discipline into accumulation**.
